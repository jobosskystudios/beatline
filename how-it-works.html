How Beatline listens

Plain JavaScript, on your Mac, in a background thread. No upload, no server, no machine-learning model. Here is each step and how it was checked.

1. Onsets: when does new sound start?

The song is mixed to mono, resampled to about 22 kHz and cut into 46 ms windows every 11.6 ms. Each window’s spectrum is grouped into 64 mel bands (spaced the way we hear pitch) and log-compressed with a knee 50 dB below the loudest moment, so the music behaves like decibels while hiss and room tone stay near zero. The onset envelope is how much each band got louder than in the previous window. The same is done for the bass band (kicks), the 150 Hz–3 kHz band (snares) and above 5 kHz (hi-hats).

Spectral flux peaks slightly before the attack you hear, so the envelope is shifted one window later. Measured on synthetic kicks, snares, hats and plucks, detected attacks land within about 5 ms of the true onset, well inside one video frame (42 ms at 23.976 fps).

2. Tempo

Beatline compares the envelope with delayed copies of itself in overlapping 8-second windows (a tempogram) and adds them up. Delays where hits line up reveal the beat period. A preference for tempos near 120 BPM settles the “70 or 140?” question, and a second check doubles a slow guess when the halfway points between beats carry real hits. The tempo chips offer the related tempos (½×, ⅔×, 1.5×, 2×) in one click.

3. Beats

Dynamic-programming beat tracking (Ellis, 2007) finds the sequence of beat times that sits on strong onsets and keeps an even spacing. Grid strictness sets how far the spacing may drift. Beats in silence before or after the music are trimmed. The tempo over time (shown as the curve under the sections) tells you whether the song is steady, a live band drifting, or changing tempo.

4. Confidence

Confidence measures how strongly the onsets repeat at the beat period, window by window. Steady music scores 0.2–0.7; speech and rubato classical score under 0.1. Below 0.12 Beatline says Unsure and suggests cutting on hits instead of a grid.

5. Time signature and bar 1

Bar starts usually bring a bass note and a chord change. Beatline scores every beat by bass attack plus how much the harmony (a 12-note chroma profile) changed, then tries 3 and 4 beats per bar at every alignment. The grouping where the strong beats line up wins. This is how it knows a waltz is in 3. Apple’s own beat detection is documented as working best in 4/4.

6. Sections and the drop

Each bar’s loudness is classified as calm, groove or peak relative to the song’s own range. Runs of bars become sections; the biggest jump up into a peak is the drop. Auto pacing uses these levels to cut slower in calm parts and faster in peaks.

Feel, accents and phrases

Swing: Beatline measures where the hits between beats fall. Straight eighths sit halfway; swung ones later (around 60–70 %); triplet feels add hits at a third and two thirds. Every 8th note pacing follows the measured swing. Accents: impacts are the hardest hits (about one per 20 s), breaks are drops of 12 dB or more lasting at least a beat, risers are bars getting brighter and louder into a louder section, and the last beat before the drop is marked. Voice phrases: speech is split at pauses longer than your setting; shorter gaps between words are joined first.

Cutting a song to length

For a target length, Beatline tries every pair of bar lines where the opening could end and the ending could start, scores each pair by how close it gets to the target, whether both are phrase or section starts, and how similar the harmony is on both sides (chroma per bar), and keeps the best. The preview and WAV use a 30 ms equal-power crossfade at the join.

Best-fitting grid

Tries six strictness settings and the tempo ±1 % and keeps the grid whose beats sit on the strongest onsets. On the evaluation songs it lifted the hardest material (a tempo-changing orchestral piece) from 0.41 to 0.63 agreement with the reference, and it runs automatically when confidence is low.

7. Key and loudness

The key is estimated by matching the song’s overall chroma against Krumhansl–Kessler major and minor profiles, shown with its Camelot code. It is an estimate and can be a related key. Loudness is integrated LUFS per ITU-R BS.1770 (K-weighting, gating), with the sample peak.

8. Frame-exact FCPXML

Final Cut stores time as exact fractions of a second. Every cut is snapped to a whole frame of your project rate and written as a fraction (frame n at 23.976 fps is n × 1001/24000 s). The song is referenced by its file path, with its real sample rate read from the file header.

<asset id="r2" name="Song" … audioChannels="2" audioRate="48000">
  <media-rep kind="original-media" src="file:///Users/you/Music/Song.wav"/>
</asset>
…
<asset-clip ref="r2" offset="0s" … audioRole="music">
  <chapter-marker start="1001/2000s" duration="1001/24000s" value="1. Intro (bars 1–8)"/>
  <marker start="1001/1000s" duration="1001/24000s" value="Bar 2"/>

How it was tested

Beyond the automated tests (synthetic drum loops at 90–150 BPM, a waltz, a C-major progression, a −20 dBFS reference tone), Beatline was scored against librosa, a widely used research library, on eight Creative Commons recordings: pop, folk, ragtime, drum & bass, a waltz, two orchestral pieces and speech. It matches the reference tempo on every steady song, and on the folk track its beats sit on stronger onsets than the reference’s. The tools are in tools/eval/ in the repository.

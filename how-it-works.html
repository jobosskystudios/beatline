How Beatline works

Everything runs in your browser with plain JavaScript — no upload, no server, no machine-learning model. Here’s the pipeline.

1. Decode

The browser’s Web Audio API decodes the file (WAV, AIFF, MP3, M4A/AAC, and FLAC where supported). Channels are averaged to mono and downsampled to about 22 kHz — plenty for finding rhythm.

2. Onset envelope: “how much new sound just started?”

The audio is cut into overlapping 46 ms windows every 11.6 ms. Each window goes through an FFT, magnitudes are log-compressed, and Beatline sums how much each frequency got louder than in the previous window (spectral flux). Drum hits, plucks and chord changes make tall spikes. A second envelope does the same for the bass band only (below 150 Hz) — that’s where kick drums live.

3. Tempo

The onset envelope is compared with delayed copies of itself (autocorrelation). Delays that line up spike-with-spike reveal the beat period. A gentle preference for tempos near 120 BPM settles the classic “is it 70 or 140?” ambiguity; if it picks the wrong one, ½× and 2× fix it.

4. Beat grid

Beatline uses dynamic-programming beat tracking (Ellis, 2007): it searches for the sequence of beat times that lands on strong onsets and keeps the spacing close to the tempo. Grid strictness sets how much each beat may deviate — high for click-tracked pop and EDM, low for live recordings. Weak beats in silent intros and outros are trimmed.

5. Downbeats

For each possible bar alignment, Beatline averages the bass-band energy on the beats that would be “beat 1”. The alignment with the most kick wins. Wrong guess? Shift it with the ◀ ▶ buttons.

Sections and the drop

Beatline measures the loudness (RMS) of every bar, smooths it, and sorts bars into three energy levels relative to the song’s own range: calm, groove and peak. Neighbouring bars at the same level become a section; sections shorter than two bars are merged into a neighbour. The biggest jump up into a peak section is labelled the drop. A calm first or last section is called the intro or outro.

Cut plan

Every exported marker inside the range is a cut. Cuts are snapped to frames, duplicates merged, and any cut that would leave a shot shorter than the shortest shot setting is skipped. Shots are the spans between cuts. Average shot length is range length ÷ shots; cuts per minute is cuts ÷ range length in minutes. Auto pacing picks a cut density per section from its energy level.

6. Hits

In Hits mode, Beatline skips the grid and marks individual peaks in the onset envelope that rise clearly above their surroundings, at least 80 ms apart. Sensitivity lowers or raises that bar.

7. Frame-exact FCPXML

Final Cut Pro stores time as exact fractions of a second. Each marker is snapped to a whole frame of your project rate, then written as a fraction — at 23.976 fps, frame n is n × 1001/24000 s. Two markers that land on the same frame are merged (downbeats win).

Accuracy

On steady music (pop, EDM, hip-hop, rock with a drummer on a click), the grid typically lands within a frame. Expect to help it with rubato piano, orchestral swells, heavy swing, or tempo changes — lower the strictness, use Hits mode, or add markers by hand.
